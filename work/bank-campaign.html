Call smarter, not more: what makes a customer say yes?

A retail bank ran phone campaigns to sell term deposits. I looked at 41,188 calls to answer two questions a campaign manager actually faces: when should we stop calling someone, and who should we call first?

Data: UCI Bank Marketing dataset, Portuguese bank, 2008–2010 (real, anonymized)Scope: 41,188 calls, 20 attributesTools: Power BI, DAX, Python

The short version

    01 · DIMINISHING RETURNS

    Every extra call is worth less than the last

    The first call converts of the time. By the fifth it's , and after ten calls it's barely .

    If the team stopped after three calls, it would make fewer calls and lose only of its sales.

    Calls here means contacts during the current campaign, including the final one.

    02 · WHO SAYS YES

    Past customers and mobile numbers convert far better

    Customers who said yes to a previous campaign said yes again of the time, against for people never contacted before.

    Calls to mobile phones converted at nearly three times the rate of landline calls. Students and retirees were the most receptive groups.

    All differences shown are significant (chi-square, p < 0.001).

    Conversion rate by customer group
    Dashed line = overall conversion rate.
    03 · TIMING

    Interest rates drove results more than anything the team did

    When the 3-month Euribor rate was under 1%, of calls converted. Above 4% it was under 5%. Customers move savings into term deposits when other options pay less.

    The busiest month, May, had the lowest conversion. Quiet months converted best, likely because those calls went to warmer, hand-picked lists.

    This is the trap of comparing months: what looks like seasonality is mostly who got called and the rate environment.

    Conversion rate by month and call volume
    Bars = calls (right axis); line = conversion rate (left axis).
    04 · WHO TO CALL FIRST

    A simple score finds 62% of sales in the top fifth of the list

    I trained a logistic regression on what's known before the call: customer profile, history and economic conditions. On customers it hadn't seen, it ranks well (AUC ).

    Calling the top 20% of the ranked list would reach of all the people who'd say yes.

    I left out call duration. It's the strongest predictor (AUC rises to ) but you only know it after the call ends, so including it would be cheating.

    What I'd recommend

    Campaign manager

    Cap attempts at three per customer

    Frees about a quarter of agent time for new contacts, at the cost of roughly one sale in eight.

    Measure: conversions per agent hour
    Marketing analytics

    Work the list in score order

    Call past customers and the top-scored segment first; collect mobile numbers at account opening.

    Measure: conversion in top 20% vs rest
    Leadership

    Plan campaign targets around the rate cycle

    Set volume and sales targets against the expected rate environment, so teams aren't judged on conditions they can't control.

    Measure: conversion vs Euribor band

    How I built it

    01 · SOURCEUCI dataset41,188 calls with outcome, customer and economic fields
    02 · PREPAREPythonGrouped calls, ages and rates into bands; checked for missing "unknown" values
    03 · TESTStatisticsChi-square tests for each driver; call-cap simulation
    04 · SCORELogistic regression70/30 split, no leakage fields, gains and AUC on hold-out
    05 · REPORTPower BIConversion drivers, call-cap what-if parameter, gains chart for the pre-call score

    Call-cap what-if in DAX

    Calls Saved =
VAR Cap = [Call Cap Value]
RETURN
    SUMX (
        Calls,
        MAX ( Calls[Total Calls] - Calls[Customers] * Cap, 0 )
    )

Sales Lost =
VAR Cap = [Call Cap Value]
RETURN
    CALCULATE (
        [Subscribed],
        Calls[Campaign Calls] > Cap
    )

Mobile vs Landline =
DIVIDE (
    CALCULATE ( [Conversion Rate], Calls[Phone] = "Mobile" ),
    CALCULATE ( [Conversion Rate], Calls[Phone] = "Landline" )
)

    Limits of this analysis

    • One bank, one product, 2008–2010; rates and channels have changed since.
    • The call-cap estimate assumes customers called more than three times wouldn't have said yes earlier.
    • Month effects are confounded with list selection; a true test would randomise call order.
    • "Unknown" values were kept as their own group rather than imputed.
