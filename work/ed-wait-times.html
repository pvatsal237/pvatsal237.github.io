Where do Ontario's emergency patients wait longest?

Ontario Health publishes how long patients spend in every hospital emergency department. I compared all 159 reporting sites for August 2026 to find where the waits pile up, and why the answer is a hospital-flow problem rather than an ED problem.

Data: Ontario Health public ED reporting, Aug 2026 (real)Scope: 159 hospital sites, 4 indicatorsTools: Power BI, DAX, Python

The short version

    01 · THE GAP

    Patients who are admitted wait more than twice the target

    Patients sent home are mostly done within the provincial targets. Patients who need a hospital bed are not: they spend an average of in the ED against an 8-hour target, and only 27% are admitted within it.

    Targets: 4 hours for low-urgency patients sent home, 8 hours for high-urgency patients sent home and for admitted patients.

    03 · THE CAUSE

    Seeing a doctor fast doesn't get you upstairs faster

    If ED staffing were the main problem, hospitals with slow first assessments would also have slow admissions. They barely do: the correlation is only . Some of the fastest EDs to see a doctor have the longest admitted waits.

    That points to the inpatient side: beds, discharges and patients waiting for another level of care.

    04 · WHERE

    GTA hospitals have the longest admitted waits

    The median GTA site keeps admitted patients in the ED for , against elsewhere in Ontario. The difference is statistically significant ().

    Most of the slowest sites are large community hospitals in fast-growing suburbs, where population growth has outpaced bed capacity.

    05 · THE TREND

    First assessment has held steady all year

    Province-wide, patients waited about two hours to see a doctor in every month of the past year, with small peaks in December and August. The front door is stable; the back door is the bottleneck.

    What I'd recommend

    Hospital leadership

    Manage admitted-patient time in the ED as a hospital-wide KPI

    Track it daily alongside discharges before 11 a.m. and patients waiting for alternate care, so inpatient units own part of the ED number.

    Measure: admitted patients within 8 h
    Ontario Health regions

    Pair the slowest sites with similar high performers

    Match each of the slowest large hospitals with a peer of similar size that is within target, and compare their bed-management practices.

    Measure: gap to peer median
    Analytics team

    Add volume and bed occupancy to the picture

    Averages hide scale. Weighting by ED volume and adding occupancy would show whether the slowest sites are simply the most crowded.

    Next step: join occupancy data

    Core measures (from the model)

    Median Admitted Hours =
VAR Sites =
    FILTER (
        VALUES ( Hospital[Hospital Key] ),
        NOT ISBLANK ( [Admitted Hours] )
    )
RETURN
    MEDIANX ( Sites, [Admitted Hours] )

Site Rank =
IF (
    HASONEVALUE ( Hospital[Hospital] ) && NOT ISBLANK ( [Admitted Hours] ),
    RANKX (
        ALLSELECTED ( Hospital[Hospital], Hospital[Area] ),
        [Admitted Hours],
        ,
        DESC
    )
)

Band Colour =
VAR B = SELECTEDVALUE ( Hospital[Admitted Band Key] )
RETURN
    SWITCH (
        TRUE (),
        B = 1, "#15803D",    -- within the 8 h target
        B >= 6, "#DC2626",   -- a day or more
        "#0D9488"
    )

    Limits of this analysis

    • One month only; seasonal patterns need more months.
    • Averages are not weighted by ED volume, so a small site counts as much as a large one.
    • The GTA grouping is mine, based on hospital location.
    • Correlation shows association, not cause; occupancy data would test the bed-flow explanation.
